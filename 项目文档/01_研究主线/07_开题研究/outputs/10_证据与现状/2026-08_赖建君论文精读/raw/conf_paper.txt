                                                         ABSTRACT

Laser processing of micro-via arrays is a critical technology in the electronic packaging industry, essential for the
rapid, non-destructive inspection of the geometric shape and depth of blind vias. As chip packaging processes
trend towards higher density and miniaturization, the demands on blind via array detection technology are
increasing. This paper proposes a fast blind via array inspection method based on dispersive spectroscopy
confocal technology. By mounting the probe on a three-axis kinematic stage, auto-focusing is achieved, enabling
rapid scanning imaging over a 10 mm × 10 mm area to acquire 3D point cloud data.

    We have developed an effective algorithm to filter noise from the 3D point cloud data and align the line scan
data, reconstructing accurate geometric profile information of the blind vias with sub-micron inspection accu-
racy. Tested on copper-clad board blind via arrays, this method quickly and accurately detects the geometric
parameters of blind vias, providing a powerful tool for real-time monitoring of blind via processing quality and
a novel solution for quality control in electronic packaging, including BGA packaging. The method offers ad-
vantages such as fast measurement speed, wide measurement range, and non-destructive, non-contact operation,
with broad application prospects in the electronics manufacturing industry. Compared to existing technologies,
our proposed measurement method is faster, offers higher resolution, and covers a wider measurement range,
meeting the increasing requirements for blind via detection in future chip packaging processes. Furthermore,
this technology can be extended to size and morphology inspection in other micro-nano processing fields, offering
significant theoretical and practical value.

Keywords: Blind Via Array Detection, Chromatic Confocal, Kalman Filter

                                                   1. INTRODUCTION

As the high-precision electronics industry rapidly evolves, sectors such as communications, aerospace, medical
devices, scientific instrumentation, and autonomous driving are imposing increasingly stringent requirements on
Printed Circuit Board (PCB) manufacturing processes. The industry is gravitating towards High Density Inter-
connectors (HDI) and Flexible Printed Circuits (FPC). Advanced packaging technologies, including Ball Grid
Array (BGA) and Chip Scale Package (CSP), necessitate precise control of laser drilling depth on copper-clad
boards to create blind via structures that connect specific layers, enabling compact three-dimensional intercon-
nect designs. This precision is crucial for the fabrication of high-density, multi-layer circuit boards.

    The quality of blind via production directly influences circuit reliability. Deviations in blind via depth from
design specifications can alter the length of interlayer connections, affecting high-frequency signal transmission
characteristics. Compromised signal integrity may lead to timing violations, crosstalk, and other issues that
degrade the PCB's electrical performance.1 For high-precision chips or BGA packages, discrepancies in blind via
depth can impede reliable connections between solder balls and PCB pads. Furthermore, tilted or defective blind
vias may damage chips or BGA ball seats.2 Laser micro-drilling has emerged as the predominant method for

    Current 3D inspection methods can be broadly categorized into two main groups: contact and non-contact
techniques. Among contact methods, the Coordinate Measuring Machine (CMM) is a prominent example, with
its resolution contingent upon the probe radius. The probe contacts multiple points on the measured surface,
effectively performing mechanical filtering, which results in a smoothed approximation of the true value. As
measurement speed increases, dynamic errors amplify, adversely affecting measurement accuracy.4 Another
contact method, Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), utilizes a probe consisting of a cantilever with a sharp tip
fixed at its end. AFM can achieve vertical resolution at the nanometer scale, surpassing the diffraction limit
of optical systems.5 Non-contact measurements primarily employ optical methods, including Confocal Laser
Microscopy (CLM), Laser Holographic Interferometry (LHI), and Structured Light Scanning (SLS). While the
resolution of optical methods is constrained by the diffraction limit, they offer greater versatility for samples
with diverse properties, rendering them more favorable in industrial applications.

    Lishchenko et al.18 developed an integrated system combining a spectral confocal sensor with a three-axis
motion stage for online measurements. Their proposed apparatus demonstrates the capability to measure surface
roughness within a range of 0.34 to 11.6 µm, encompassing the typical range encountered in milling, turning,
and grinding processes. The system maintains a relative measurement error of less than 10%. The researchers
utilized the measurement results to implement process monitoring diagnostics based on profile estimation.

    The utilization of multiple spectral confocal sensors enables simultaneous acquisition of surface topography in
various dimensions or the execution of complex measurements. Wang et al.19 implemented a dual-probe method
for roundness measurements using two chromatic confocal sensors. In their study, repeated measurements of a
workpiece yielded an average roundness of 0.53 µm, with a mere 0.04 µm deviation when compared to results from

                       Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13241 132411W-2
a high-precision roundness meter. These findings demonstrate that roundness measurement systems employing
confocal sensors can provide measurements of considerable accuracy.

    Pillarz et al.20 developed an optical gear shape measurement system utilizing spectral confocal sensors. The
system achieves a standard measurement uncertainty of less than 6.5 µm, fulfilling the requirements for micron-
level precision. Compared to Coordinate Measuring Machines (CMM) and Gear Measuring Instruments (GMI),
this system significantly enhances measurement speed and overcomes the limitations of traditional methods,
which are unable to measure each gear tooth individually.

    This paper proposes an automated 3D surface measurement system based on dispersive spectral confocal tech-
nology, which integrates a three-axis motion stage and 3D point cloud data processing techniques. By employing
Kalman filtering and Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), the system effectively minimizes measurement-
induced errors and distinguishes copper-clad board deformation data from blind via depth data.

In LSCM, two pinholes, positioned at the illumination and detection points, are conjugated, allowing for point
illumination and the exclusion of out-of-focus background signals. The detector receives the maximum reflected
light intensity only when the surface being measured aligns with the focal plane of the focused light spot.

    To determine the position of the surface being measured, a piezoelectric ceramic (PZT) is employed to drive
the microscope objective during scanning. The measurement range, speed, precision, and other parameters of
Laser Scanning Confocal Microscopy (LSCM) are dependent on the high-precision motion control of the PZT.
As a result, the entire scanning system is structurally complex and costly.

    To obtain axial position information more efficiently, Molesini et al.23 proposed a wavelength-encoded pro-
filometer model, which utilizes axial chromatic dispersion elements to encode axial positions. Fig. 3 illustrates
the principle of chromatic confocal measurement. A broadband light source (e.g., white light) is passed through a
chromatic objective lens, where chromatic dispersion causes shorter wavelengths (e.g., blue light) to have shorter
focal lengths, converging above the sample, while longer wavelengths (e.g., red light) have longer focal lengths,
converging below the sample. A pinhole in front of the spectral analyzer ensures that only information from
the focal plane is received. By analyzing the central wavelength of the detected signal, the axial position of the
measured point is determined through a lookup table, effectively encoding axial information within the spectrum.

                      Figure 3. Schematic diagram of dispersive confocal (spectral confocal) measurement.24
    Chromatic confocal technology allows for rapid and precise axial positioning without requiring axial scanning.
The axial detection range and resolution are governed by the chromatic dispersion element and the spectral
resolution of the detector. The detection range can span from a few millimeters to several tens of millimeters,
while resolution capabilities range from a few nanometers to several hundred nanometers.

2.2 Fundamentals of Kalman Filtering
The Kalman filter (KF) is an optimized, recursive data processing algorithm, also referred to as linear quadratic
estimation. It predicts a system's future state based on its past and current states.25 What distinguishes the
Kalman filter from most other filters is its exclusive operation in the time domain. Unlike frequency-domain
filters, such as low-pass filters, which are designed in the frequency domain and then converted to the time
domain for implementation, the Kalman filter directly operates within the time domain.

    The state of the filter is represented by two variables: x^k|k, the a posteriori state estimate mean at time k
given observations up to and including at time k; Pk|k, the a posteriori estimate covariance matrix (a measure
of the estimated accuracy of the state estimate). The algorithm operates in two stages: predict and update.
The predict phase uses the state estimate from the previous timestep to produce an estimate of the state at the

    In this model, the input signal is the reflected spectrum, and there are no external factors influencing signal
acquisition; therefore, the input matrix Bk is set to a zero matrix. Since the observed variable is one-dimensional
and exhibits relatively small changes, the state-transition matrix Fk is set to an identity matrix. Additionally,
because the observed value directly corresponds to the system state, the observation matrix Hk is also set to an
identity matrix.

    Process noise and measurement noise are critical parameters in the Kalman filter. In real-world systems,
noise characteristics may vary over time. Dynamically adjusting the process noise covariance matrix Q and
measurement noise covariance matrix R using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) allows the filter to adapt to these
changes, rather than relying on fixed a priori estimates. By minimizing MAE, the optimal values for Q and
R can be identified for a given dataset, thereby enhancing the overall accuracy of state estimation. MAE, as
a performance metric, is less sensitive to outliers compared to Mean Squared Error (MSE), and may be more
effective in handling non-Gaussian noise.

    As shown in Fig. 6, the Kalman filter effectively reduces measurement noise. In the raw data, some anomalous
data points appear at the edges of the blind vias. These anomalies arise because the detection light spot has
a specific size; when it simultaneously covers both the copper-clad board surface and the blind via, calculation
errors occur. The Kalman filter can, to some extent, correct these anomalous data points.

                                                     4. CONCLUSION
By utilizing a spectral confocal probe in combination with a three-dimensional motion platform, rapid scanning
and precise reconstruction of blind hole arrays can be achieved. This approach is pivotal in PCB manufacturing
and inspection, as it greatly improves both the efficiency and accuracy of the inspection process. In terms
of data processing, the Kalman filter, when compared to traditional median or low-pass filters, not only offers
excellent real-time performance but also demonstrates strong robustness. The Kalman filter effectively suppresses
anomalous measurement data, thereby enhancing the system's overall stability and reliability. In conclusion, the
integration of the spectral confocal probe with the Kalman filter provides an efficient and dependable solution
for PCB inspection.
